# Field Capture resource budget decision

## Recommended decision

The right design decision for **Field Capture** is to make the **conservative tier the default support contract** and enable the **comfortable tier only on better phones** after runtime checks. The reason is straightforward: the low end of the real Android market that blue-collar BYOD users may still carry in 2024-2026 is still very much alive at **4 GB RAM / 64 GB storage** on devices like the **Moto G Play 2024**, while common budget Samsung phones sit at **4 GB RAM / 64-128 GB storage** and newer budget Motorola devices at **4 GB RAM / 128 GB**. Samsung and Motorola are also the most relevant Android brands to watch in the US and prepaid channels, which matters for field-worker BYOD reality more than flagship-market averages do. citeturn19search4turn16search5turn16search4turn16search3turn20search11turn15search0turn15search9turn15search13turn15search1

New 128 GB budget Samsungs can expose roughly **108-109 GB available** out of the box, but that is not the planning baseline for a field app on personal phones. A BYOD field app has to survive the user who already has family photos, messaging media, social apps, and limited free space. Because **64 GB devices remain on sale** and Android specifically warns developers that many users have little available storage and that app-specific internal storage “tends to be small,” Field Capture should treat **4 GB RAM / 64 GB storage** as the hard floor and budget itself accordingly. citeturn16search0turn16search3turn19search4turn39view0

### Budget table

| Budget item                                      | Conservative tier | Comfortable tier |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------: | ---------------: |
| **Total on-disk app/cache hard cap**             |        **1.5 GB** |       **3.0 GB** |
| SQLite / structured data                         |             64 MB |           128 MB |
| Photos / images                                  |            384 MB |           1.0 GB |
| Print-job queue and rendered payloads            |             32 MB |            64 MB |
| App binary / installed footprint                 |            120 MB |           160 MB |

| Camera / photo capture RAM target                |            200 MB |           280 MB |
| Background sync RAM target                       |             60 MB |            90 MB |
| Print rendering / printing RAM target            |             80 MB |           120 MB |
| **Cellular per sync cycle cap**                  |          **1 MB** |         **2 MB** |

These numbers are intentionally **self-imposed quotas**, not operating-system quotas. Android gives apps app-specific persistent and cache directories, a cache-quota API, and allocatable-bytes checks, while iOS similarly expects developers to separate durable data from purgeable cache data and lets apps query storage capacity for important usage. In practice, neither platform gives you a simple “safe per-app storage allowance,” so the correct architecture is to impose your own budget and make purgeability explicit by data class. citeturn39view0turn21search0turn21search7turn38search2turn38search14

My recommendation is to unlock the **comfortable tier** only when all of the following are true: **device RAM >= 6 GB, device storage >= 128 GB, Android `isLowRamDevice()` is false, and important free storage >= 10 GB**. Everything else should remain on the conservative tier. Confidence on the conservative tier is **high**. Confidence on the comfortable-tier gating thresholds is **medium**, because those are design cutoffs rather than published vendor thresholds. citeturn24search13turn24search16turn39view0

## Hardware floor and platform constraints

A realistic Android floor for this project in 2026 is **Android 11+ officially supported**, optimized hard for **Android 13 and 14** low-end devices. The representative budget devices in scope are not theoretical: Motorola sells the **Moto G Play 2024** with **Android 13, Snapdragon 680, 4 GB RAM, and 64 GB storage**; Motorola also sells the **Moto G 5G 2024** with **Android 14, Snapdragon 4 Gen 1, 4 GB RAM, and 128 GB storage**; Samsung’s budget Galaxy A line includes configurations such as **Galaxy A15 5G 4 GB / 64-128 GB** and **Galaxy A16 5G 4 GB / 128 GB**. That means the app must be comfortable on modest octa-core CPUs with constrained memory bandwidth and no assumption of flagship thermal headroom. citeturn19search4turn20search5turn20search11turn16search4turn16search3turn20search7

Android’s memory model makes this especially important. Google documents that Android tries to use available RAM aggressively, then reclaims under pressure with `kswapd`, zRAM, app `onTrimMemory()` callbacks, and finally the low-memory killer daemon. Background and cached processes die first; foreground and perceptible processes are more protected but not immune. Google also now treats a **user-perceived LMK rate above 1% as a critical signal** in Android vitals. In other words, an app that carries around too many decoded images or concurrent upload buffers will not merely feel slower; it will get killed more often. citeturn23view2turn23view0turn23view1turn24search0

Google’s current low-resource guidance also matters here. The Build for Billions guidance still tells developers to make apps run smoothly on devices with **<=1 GB RAM**, and Android’s low-RAM device guidance says apps should scale down behavior based on `isLowRamDevice()` and memory class. Even though Field Capture does not need to support Android Go-class hardware as a primary target, those documents make the directional rule clear: **do not design by flagship assumptions**. citeturn24search10turn24search16turn24search13

On iOS, the storage and memory picture is different but points to the same operational conclusion. Apple expects durable data to live in persistent app directories and **purgeable data** to live in **Caches** or **tmp**; Apple explicitly notes that the system may purge the Caches directory when the app is not running and uses memory warnings and jetsam reports when memory pressure is high. Apple also says the memory level that triggers warnings depends on device conditions, and background work is not guaranteed to run for long. That means the same storage partitioning and conservative background RAM goals are correct on iPhone too, even if the immediate priority for this project is Android. citeturn38search1turn38search2turn38search14turn25search7turn25search1turn37search11turn37search18turn37search0

## Evidence behind each storage class

**Structured data / SQLite.** Role-scoped service requests, tickets, JHA/JSA forms, employee/reference data, permissions, mutation queues, and printer-job metadata are all excellent fits for internal app-specific persistent storage. Android explicitly recommends internal app storage for sensitive app-only data, warns that internal storage is limited, and provides `getAllocatableBytes()` and related APIs to check space. Because Field Capture is role-scoped and Hub remains the source of truth, **64 MB conservative / 128 MB comfortable** is enough for structured state if large binary payloads stay out of SQLite and only compact metadata, signatures-as-strokes, and file references live there. Confidence: **high**. citeturn39view0

**Photos and image handling.** Google ML Kit says text-recognition input should ideally give each character about **16 x 16 pixels**, with little benefit above **24 x 24**, and notes that a **720 x 1280** image may be enough for a letter-size document photo. Google also recommends dynamic image sizing because it reduces both network transfer and memory usage. That strongly supports a deliberate capture pipeline rather than “store whatever the camera produced.” My recommendation is to normalize captured field-ticket and receipt photos to a **single durable canonical image at about a 1600 px long edge** and **not keep both full originals and compressed copies** unless you later discover a legal or evidentiary requirement that genuinely requires originals. That is enough for readable records and OCR headroom, while keeping decoded bitmap pressure manageable. Confidence: **medium-high**. citeturn7search0turn7search4turn35view1

The memory reason for downscaling is strong. Android’s own bitmap guidance reminds developers that a single **4048 x 3036** ARGB bitmap can consume about **48 MB** in memory, and that most ARGB bitmaps cost **4 bytes per pixel**. A 1600 x 1200 decoded image is only about **7.3 MB** in ARGB, which is far more compatible with low-end devices when you account for preview, crop, thumbnail, and upload buffers. That is why the camera path gets a separate RAM target of **200 MB conservative / 280 MB comfortable**, and why uploads should be **serialized** rather than batched heavily. Confidence: **high**. citeturn33search0turn33search4turn33search2

For durable photo encoding, the format tradeoff is real. Google’s own WebP studies show **25%-34% smaller** files than JPEG at equivalent SSIM quality, and Android’s low-data guidance specifically recommends WebP for photos to reduce bandwidth. However, given that Ops Hub is a self-hosted FastAPI/React system on an office Windows PC and this is an operations app rather than a media product, my default recommendation is to use **JPEG as the authoritative durable format** for field photos because it has the lowest operational surprise across printers, office previews, exports, and support workflows. If your backend and browser pipeline are fully verified for WebP end-to-end, switching canonical image storage to WebP is a valid optimization that effectively enlarges the photo budget without changing the hard cap. The storage budgets above assume **JPEG-quality canonical photos averaging roughly 0.75 MB each**; that per-photo number is an engineering estimate, not a published vendor figure, so confidence there is **medium**. citeturn28view0turn35view0turn35view1

**Print-job queue and rendered ticket payloads.** This category is much smaller than photos if it is designed correctly. If a job stores metadata plus **ESC/POS text bytes**, the payload is only a few kilobytes. Even if you store a ready-to-print **1-bit 384-dot bitmap fallback**, the storage is still modest: a 384 px wide bitmap at roughly 1760 px tall is only about **84 KB raw** at 1 bit per pixel, and even a 2400 px tall render is only about **115 KB raw**. A small signature bitmap is tiny beside that. That means a **32 MB conservative queue budget** easily supports **200+ unprinted or unsynced jobs**, and **64 MB comfortable** supports **500+** with room for retries and status metadata. Confidence: **high**, because the dominant numbers are math rather than unknown vendor behavior.

That is also why the durability rule should be strict: **store the print job durably only after its data payload is finalized**, keep it until it is both **printed successfully** and **Hub-synced**, and then retain a compact local history for a short audit window. This aligns with the project rule that the phone is not the authority but must not silently lose unprinted high-value records.

## Memory and cellular budgets

The RAM targets above are deliberately lower than what many modern phones could tolerate, because background-kill risk is created by **practical working set**, not advertised RAM. Android documents that cached processes can be killed at any time and that background services can be demoted; Apple documents that background execution is heuristic-driven and that the system most often terminates apps in the background when a foreground app needs memory. That means the correct budget is not “how much can a Galaxy S26 survive,” but “how small can Field Capture stay and still do its work on a 4 GB BYOD Android phone without warm-start loss and background-sync flakiness.” Confidence: **medium-high**. citeturn23view1turn23view0turn37search11turn37search18turn37search0

The main RAM trap is image concurrency. ODK’s own documentation notes that when form submissions with media are uploaded or blank forms with media are downloaded, **all associated media files can be held in memory at the same time, twice**. That is a strong warning against multi-photo parallel uploads on low-end field devices. For Field Capture, the safest design is **one large photo transcode/upload at a time**, or at most **two** on comfortable-tier devices. That single decision supports the proposed **60 MB conservative / 90 MB comfortable** background-sync target and makes RAM behavior much more deterministic. Confidence: **high**. citeturn27search7

The cellular-data budget should also be intentionally frugal, because the phones are personal devices. Google’s Build for Billions guidance emphasizes that data plans can be financially meaningful and recommends giving users control over sync and network behavior. US prepaid plans still visibly market **5 GB** monthly tiers, so a **15 MB/day conservative target** keeps Field Capture around **450 MB/month**, which is meaningfully respectful of the worker’s own plan while still leaving space for text sync, metadata refresh, and occasional urgent photo transfer. Confidence: **medium-high**. citeturn35view0turn35view1turn36search4

That is why my concrete cellular policy is this: **ordinary record sync must stay under 1 MB per cycle conservative / 2 MB comfortable; photo uploads over cellular should be staggered and rate-limited; and emergency days may exceed the normal target but should still stay under 60 MB conservative / 120 MB comfortable unless the user explicitly overrides for urgent photo transfer**. This fits both the offline-first design and the user-paid-phone reality. Confidence: **medium**, because daily photo volume varies by operation.

## Eviction and retention policy

The eviction model should be simple, explicit, and ruthless about what is safe to delete.

The **first things deleted** should be **logs and diagnostics**, then **temporary transcode files**, then **generated thumbnails and other fully regenerable cache**. Android explicitly expects apps to bound their cache in cache directories and not rely on the system to manage it for them, and Apple similarly tells developers to put purgeable data in Caches/tmp because those locations can be reclaimed. Confidence: **high**. citeturn39view0turn38search1turn38search2turn38search14

The following items should **never be silently evicted** before Hub acknowledgment: **unsynced field-ticket photos**, **unsynced disposal/receipt photos**, **unprinted print jobs**, **printed-but-unsynced print jobs**, and **offline mutations that have not been accepted by Ops Hub**. That rule follows directly from the product requirements and from the general offline-field pattern seen in tools like ODK and ArcGIS Field Maps, where work remains on-device until sync succeeds. Confidence: **high**. citeturn27search3turn27search5turn27search20turn14search5

For **photos**, I recommend this lifecycle. Capture into a temporary file, immediately normalize to the single durable canonical upload image, verify it, then delete the temporary raw capture. Retain the canonical image until Hub confirms receipt. After Hub acknowledgment, keep the full photo locally for **7 days on conservative tier** and **14 days on comfortable tier**, then purge the full-resolution local copy and retain only metadata plus a small thumbnail. If the device enters pressure mode earlier, Hub-synced full photos may be purged sooner. Confidence: **medium-high**.

For **print jobs**, retain **all unprinted or unsynced jobs indefinitely until both print success and Hub sync occur**. After that, keep a local history of the most recent **100 jobs or 14 days** on conservative tier, and **300 jobs or 30 days** on comfortable tier, whichever is smaller. Because the print queue is relatively cheap in storage, the main objective here is durability and replayability, not aggressive space recovery. Confidence: **high**.

For **logs**, use a ring buffer and hard cap them at **16 MB conservative / 32 MB comfortable**, with age-based purge at **7 days conservative / 14 days comfortable**. Confidence: **high**.

### Cleanup thresholds

| Threshold        |                           Conservative tier |                            Comfortable tier |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------: | ------------------------------------------: |
| **Warning**      |  1.0 GB used or important free space < 4 GB |  2.1 GB used or important free space < 6 GB |
| **Soft cleanup** | 1.25 GB used or important free space < 2 GB | 2.55 GB used or important free space < 4 GB |
| **Hard cap**     |  1.5 GB used or important free space < 1 GB |  3.0 GB used or important free space < 2 GB |

When the app reaches the **warning** threshold, it should notify the user and surface storage details. At **soft cleanup**, it should automatically purge only regenerable caches and old synced photos according to the policies above. At the **hard cap**, it must stop accepting new optional large attachments and block any new photo capture or print-job generation that cannot be durably committed first. Android and Apple both provide APIs intended for exactly this kind of “important usage” storage check. Confidence: **high**. citeturn39view0turn21search0turn21search7

## Red flags, confidence, and limitations

If Field Capture exceeds the storage budget, the failure mode is not subtle. The user will see **camera capture failures, stuck sync, SQLite “disk full” style behavior, and durable-queue write failures**. Android explicitly warns that many devices have little available storage and that apps should query free space before writing; Apple similarly distinguishes important storage capacity from opportunistic capacity. Storage exhaustion is therefore a product reliability bug, not an edge case. citeturn39view0turn21search0turn21search7

If the RAM targets are exceeded, the symptoms will be **slow image handling, app restarts when multitasking, and background sync/print interruptions**. On Android, that shows up as elevated LMK activity, and Google treats **user-perceived LMK > 1%** as a critical signal. On iOS, the same problem appears as memory warnings, jetsam terminations, or background execution that simply does not survive long enough to finish. Confidence: **high on the risk, medium on exact per-device thresholds**, because Apple does not publish universal hard numbers for all devices. citeturn24search0turn23view0turn23view1turn25search7turn25search1turn37search11

The highest-confidence recommendations in this report are: **the 4 GB / 64 GB Android hardware floor; the need for strict storage partitioning; photo budgets as the dominant storage class; serialized media sync; and the rule that unsynced photos and unprinted/unsynced print jobs must never be silently evicted**. Confidence is lower, but still usable, for the exact **per-photo average byte estimate**, because it depends on field capture quality and the final image normalization pipeline. citeturn19search4turn16search5turn16search4turn39view0turn28view0
